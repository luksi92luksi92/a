when will it be posible to distinguish pad from drum or similar eg same instrument or sampled sound


Claude:
Two different questions bundled together there, and they need genuinely different machinery:
1. "Pad vs. drum" — sustained vs. percussive is the easier one. It doesn't need embeddings or ML at all — it's a timbre-class label derivable from features the physical layer already half-computes:

Attack time: how fast energy rises to peak. Pads: tens–hundreds of ms. Drums: <10ms. (Currently crest_factor hints at this but nothing measures rise time explicitly.)
Sustain ratio: energy in the tail (>200ms after onset) vs. energy in the first 50ms. Percussive sounds decay away; pads don't.
Harmonic stability over the object's own lifetime: does the tracked frequency sit still for a long time (pad) or does the whole spectral shape evolve rapidly within a couple hundred ms and then go silent (drum)? The object's history array already has everything needed for this — it's just not being read yet.
Spectral flatness trend: drums (especially unpitched ones) tend toward noisier onsets even if the body is tonal.

This would live as a new timbre_class field on SoundObject (with its own confidence, computed once an object has enough history — say >150ms of lifetime), populated by a small rule-based classifier module sitting after the tracker. This is a genuinely tractable next addition to the current code — no architecture change needed, just a new module reading obj.history.
2. "Same instrument" / "same sampled sound" across different objects is the harder one, because it's not a tracking problem (one object persisting through time) — it's a similarity problem across objects that don't overlap in time and may even be at different pitches. That needs a pitch-normalized timbre fingerprint:

Take the spectral envelope, strip out the fundamental/harmonic spacing (e.g. cepstral liftering — this is basically what MFCCs do), and you get a vector that describes timbre independent of pitch.
Store that vector per object once it stabilizes.
Add a new relationship type — SAME_SOURCE — computed by comparing timbre-vector distance between objects, using the relationship graph machinery that's already in place (typed edges, confidence, evidence accumulation over repeated hits).
"Exact same sample" is a stricter version of this — closer to audio fingerprinting (Shazam-style constellation matching) than timbre classification, since two different real drum samples of "a kick" will have similar timbre-class but different fine spectral fingerprints.

Rule-based MFCC-distance clustering gets you a decent version of #2 without deep learning. A learned embedding (small CNN/autoencoder over log-mel frames) gets you a more robust version that generalizes across velocity/articulation — that's the "embeddings" work I deliberately deferred, and it's real additional scope, not a small bolt-on.

Input sounds: kick, bass, hi-hat, pad — 4 sources, fixed in the synthetic generator.
Why they're not each one clean layer, in order of impact:

Kick and bass share the same bark band (both ~35–180Hz) — the peak-picker can only report one dominant peak per band per hop, so they compete rather than both reliably generating separate evidence.
Hi-hat is broadband noise, so it spreads across many bark bands and spawns many separate fragment-objects (one per band it happens to peak in that hop) instead of one object.
GroupingEngine only merges what it empirically sees co-firing above the confirmation bar (rate ≥50%, same-hop, sustained) — it has no semantic knowledge that "these fragments are all hi-hat." It'll merge a genuinely-correlated subset, not necessarily every fragment belonging to the same true source.
This is the known, already-discussed limitation of a bark-band peak-picker doing what's effectively no-ML source separation — it's literally why HTDemucs came up as an auxiliary cue several turns back. Without it, "one true source → one clean object/layer" isn't guaranteed.
